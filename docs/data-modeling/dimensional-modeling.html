04

Why dimensional modeling · the core idea

Dimensional modeling in one sentence

Store each business process as a table of measurements, surrounded by tables of descriptions you can filter and group by.

01Fact tables hold measurements

Each row records a business event — a sale, a shipment, a class booking — with its numbers: quantity, amount, duration. Fact tables are long and narrow: many rows, few columns.

02Dimension tables hold descriptions

Each row describes a “who, what, where, when”: a customer, a product, a date, a warehouse. Dimension tables are short and wide: fewer rows, many descriptive columns.

03Keys connect them

Every fact row stores a key for each dimension that describes it. To answer a question you join the fact table to the dimensions you need, then group and sum.

The approach was popularised by Ralph Kimball in The Data Warehouse Toolkit (1996, third edition 2013) and is still the most common way analytics data is organised — in warehouses such as Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks and Fabric, and in BI tools such as Power BI and Tableau.

The main idea

measurements in the middle, descriptions around the edge. Every technique in this course is a refinement of that one idea.

05

Building blocks · measurements vs context

Facts vs dimensions

A fact is a measurement of an event

Something happened in the business, and we measured it. Facts are almost always numbers, and usually you want to add them up.

Leafy examples: quantity = 2, line_amount = 24.00, shipping_fee = 8.00.

A dimension gives that event its context

Dimensions answer who, what, where, when, how, and why. They are mostly text, and you use them to filter, group, and label results.

Leafy examples: the product Ceramic Pot in category Accessories, the customer Maya in Austin, the date Monday 2 March 2026.

Sort each column from Leafy’s order lines:

ColumnFact or dimension?Why
line_amountFact (measure)A number produced by the sale; summing it is meaningful.
quantityFact (measure)A count of units; summing it gives units sold.
product name, categoryDimension attributeText that describes what was sold; you group by it.

order_dateDimension (date)Describes when; you group by month, weekday, quarter.

ask “is this a measurement taken when the event happened, or a description of something involved in it?” Numbers that describe a thing (weight, list price) usually belong in the dimension.

06

Building blocks · a habit that makes design easy

Hear the facts and dimensions in a question

Business questions almost always follow the same shape: a number, then “by” one or more descriptions, sometimes with a filter. The number is a fact; the “by” words and filters are dimensions.

Business questionFact (the number)Dimensions (by / for / where)
Revenue by category by monthrevenue = SUM(line_amount)product → category; date → month
Units sold in Denver last weekSUM(quantity)customer → city; date → week
Average order value by marketing channelSUM(amount) ÷ COUNT(orders)order profile → channel
How many orders shipped late from each warehouse?COUNT of late orderswarehouse; date (ship vs promised)

Why this matters

When you interview stakeholders, write their questions down and underline the numbers and the “by” words. The numbers become columns in fact tables; the “by” words become dimension tables and their attributes.

A useful rule of thumb

If people say “by X” or “for each X” or “only X”, then X belongs in a dimension. If they say “how much” or “how many”, that is a fact.

The main idea

a dimensional model is simply the shape of the questions people ask: measures to aggregate, descriptions to slice by.

Building blocks · what goes in, what stays out

Anatomy of a fact table

Leafy’s order-line fact table

fact_order_linefact

One row per order line. Four rows for our sample orders.

Three kinds of column

  • Foreign keys (FK) — one per dimension that describes the event. Together they say who, what, where, when.
  • Measures (M) — the numbers recorded by the event. These are what you SUM, COUNT, or AVG.
  • Degenerate dimensions (DD) — transaction identifiers like the order number. Lesson 35 explains these.

What stays out: descriptive text. The product name is not repeated on every sale row; it lives once in the product dimension and is reached through product_key.

Fact tables are the biggest tables in a warehouse — often billions of rows — so they are kept narrow: integer keys and numbers.

The main idea

a fact row = keys to its context + the numbers measured. If you find yourself adding a text description to a fact table, it probably belongs in a dimension.

Design habits

  • Surrogate key (PK) — a warehouse-made number such as 1, 2, 3. Lesson 20 explains why.
  • Natural key (NK) — the ID from the source system, kept so you can trace a row back.
  • Flattened hierarchies — product → subcategory → category all sit in one row, even though “Plants” repeats. Repeating short text is cheap; extra joins are not.
  • Readable labels, not codes — store “Yes / No” or “Pet safe / Not pet safe” instead of 1 / 0, and “Shipped” instead of status code 3. Report labels come straight from these columns.
  • Many attributes — dimensions often have dozens of columns. Each one is a new way to slice the facts.
The main idea

a good dimension reads like a well-labelled spreadsheet: one row per thing, every useful description as its own column, nothing hidden behind codes or extra joins.

Why analytics prefers a star

One table. “Revenue by category” is one join from the fact table. “Plants” repeats on several rows — that is fine.

  • subcategory_keyFK
dim_subcategorydimension
  • subcategory_keyPK
  • subcategory

Three tables. Less repetition, but “revenue by category” now needs three joins, and every user must know the chain.

Simple to understand

Business users can see the whole model on one page. Table names match how people talk: sales, products, customers, dates.

Simple to query

Every question has the same shape: join the fact to a few dimensions, filter, group, sum. Fewer joins also means fewer ways to make mistakes.

Fast

Engines and BI tools are optimised for this shape (“star join” plans, columnar compression of repeated text). Power BI’s own guidance recommends star schemas.

A snowflake schema normalizes dimensions into sub-tables. It is not wrong, and sometimes useful for very large shared hierarchies, but it gives up simplicity for storage savings that rarely matter today. A one big table (everything flattened into the fact) is easy to query, but repeats descriptions on every row and makes history and reuse hard; teams often build it from a star.

The main idea

prefer a star by default: flatten hierarchies into the dimension and keep every dimension one join from the facts.

Building blocks · join, filter, group, aggregate

Every star query has the same shape

Revenue by category for March 2026

SQL
SELECT
    p.category,
    SUM(f.line_amount) AS revenue,
    SUM(f.quantity)    AS units
FROM fact_order_line AS f
JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
JOIN dim_date    AS d ON d.date_key    = f.date_key
WHERE d.year = 2026
  AND d.month_name = 'March'
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY revenue DESC;
categoryrevenueunits
Plants65.002
Accessories42.005

Read it as a recipe

  1. FROM the fact table — start from the measurements.
  2. JOIN each dimension you need, on its key.
  3. WHERE on dimension attributes — the filters.
  4. GROUP BY dimension attributes — the “by” words.
  5. SUM / COUNT the measures.

Check it: Plants = Monstera 40 + Snake Plant 25 = 65. Accessories = Ceramic Pot 24 + Fertilizer 18 = 42. Total 107, matching lesson 3.

BI tools such as Power BI generate exactly this query when someone drags “category” and “revenue” onto a chart.

The main idea

if your model is a proper star, every business question becomes the same five-step query. That consistency is the real payoff.

Grain · what does one row represent?

Grain: the most important decision

The grain of a fact table is a precise statement of what one row represents.

Grain statementExample fact tableRows for Leafy’s sample
One row per orderfact_order3 (SO-5001, SO-5002, SO-5003)
One row per order line (product within an order)fact_order_line4
One row per product per warehouse per dayfact_inventory_dailyproducts × warehouses × days
One row per customer per dayfact_customer_daily_activitycustomers × active days
One row per website sessionfact_web_sessionone per visit

Why it comes first

The grain decides which dimensions can attach to a row and which measures make sense on it. A product can describe an order line, but not a whole order that contains three products.

How to say it well

Write it in business words, as a sentence, before choosing any columns: “One row per product on a customer order.” If two people read it and imagine different rows, it is not precise enough.

The main idea

if you remember one thing from this course: before designing a fact table, finish the sentence “one row in this table represents one …”. Interviewers ask this constantly.

14

Grain · atomic, declared first, never mixed

Three rules for grain, and the classic mistake

01Prefer the atomic grain

Store the most detailed level the source provides (order line, not order; individual event, not daily total). It answers the widest range of questions and survives new requirements. Build summaries on top if speed is needed.

02Declare it before columns

Write the grain sentence first. Then a dimension is allowed only if it has a single value for that row; a measure only if it is true for exactly that row.

03Never mix grains

Every row in one fact table must represent the same kind of thing. Facts recorded at a different level go in a different fact table.

The mistake: order-level shipping on line rows

order_numberlineline_amountshipping_fee
SO-5001140.008.00
SO-5001224.008.00
SO-5002125.005.00
SO-5003118.000.00

The shipping fee belongs to the order, but it was copied onto each line. SUM(shipping_fee) returns 21; the true total is 13 (8 + 5 + 0).

Two correct fixes

Allocate it down to the line grain, for example by each line’s share of the order amount: Monstera gets 8 × 40/64 = 5.00, Ceramic Pot 8 × 24/64 = 3.00. Now every column is true for its row, and sums are correct.

Or keep a separate order-grain fact table (fact_order) for order-level measures such as shipping fee and order-level discounts.

Either way, document the choice so nobody adds the order-level number back onto lines later.

The main idea

double counting almost always comes from a measure stored at the wrong grain. Ask “is this number true for exactly one row?”

Grain · how every dimensional model is designed

The four-step design process

Kimball’s four steps give you an order to make decisions in. They work for a one-table project and for an enterprise warehouse, and they are a great structure for answering “design a data model for…” interview questions.

01
Choose the business processa verb: take orders, ship parcels, book classes

An operational activity that generates events you can measure. One process → (usually) one fact table.

02
Declare the grain“one row per order line”

The precise meaning of one row. Decided before anything else; it constrains the next two steps.

03
Identify the dimensionswho, what, where, when, how, why

Descriptions that have exactly one value for a row at this grain: date, customer, product, warehouse.

04
Identify the factsthe numbers measured

Measurements true at this grain: quantity, line amount, discount. Anything at another grain goes elsewhere.

Leafy, worked through

Process: customer orders. Grain: one row per product on an order. Dimensions: order date, customer, product, warehouse, promotion. Facts: quantity, unit price, line amount, discount amount.

Start from requirements and data together

Collect the business questions (lesson 7) and look at what the source systems actually record. The best design satisfies the questions using data that really exists.

The main idea

process → grain → dimensions → facts. Say these four steps out loud in any modeling interview and you will already sound structured.

Measures · which numbers can be added up, and along which dimensions

Three kinds of measures

Not every number in a fact table can simply be summed. Classify each measure by whether SUM gives a meaningful answer across all, some, or none of the dimensions.

KindCan be summed across…Leafy examplesHow to aggregate
Additiveevery dimension (products, customers, dates…)quantity, line_amount, discount_amount, shipping_fee (at the right grain)SUM, freely
Semi-additivesome dimensions, but not timeunits on hand in a warehouse; a customer’s gift-card balanceSUM across products or warehouses; across time use the period-end value, or an average
Non-additiveno dimensionunit_price, discount %, margin %, conversion rate, distinct customer countsStore the additive parts; compute the ratio after summing

Why it matters

A BI tool will happily SUM any number column. If a measure is semi- or non-additive, the default total on a report is wrong unless the model or the measure definition prevents it.

Best practice

Prefer additive measures. Store numerators and denominators (margin_amount, sales_amount) rather than ratios. Document semi-additive measures and define their aggregation explicitly in the semantic layer.

The main idea

additive: sum anything. Semi-additive: sum anything except time. Non-additive: never sum — rebuild from additive parts.

Measures · stock levels and balances

Semi-additive measures in action

Leafy records how many Monsteras are on the shelf at the end of each day in each warehouse. That number is a level (a state at a moment), not an activity (something that happened during the period).

datewarehouseunits_on_handunits_sold
03-01Austin100
03-01Denver40
03-02Austin91
03-02Denver40
03-03Austin90
03-03Denver31
Ask a question

“How many Monsteras did we have on 3 March?” Austin 9 + Denver 3 = 12. Summing a level across warehouses (or products) on the same day is correct.

“How many Monsteras did Austin have in the first three days of March?” SUM gives 10 + 9 + 9 = 28. Nonsense: there were never 28 plants. Use the last value (9, the closing stock) or the average (9.33).

units_sold is an activity, so it is fully additive: Austin sold 1 and Denver sold 1 → 2 over the three days. Snapshots often carry both kinds of measure side by side.

SQL
-- Closing stock per warehouse for March: take the last day of the period
SELECT w.warehouse_name, SUM(f.units_on_hand) AS closing_units
FROM fact_inventory_daily AS f
JOIN dim_warehouse AS w ON w.warehouse_key = f.warehouse_key
JOIN dim_date      AS d ON d.date_key      = f.date_key
WHERE d.full_date = DATE '2026-03-31'      -- one day only: no summing over time
GROUP BY w.warehouse_name;
The main idea

balances, stock levels, headcounts and queue lengths are semi-additive: sum them across things, but pick one point in time (or average) across time.

Measures · ratios, percentages, prices, and distinct counts

Non-additive measures: store the parts

The trap: averaging percentages

ordersales_amountmargin_amountmargin_%
A100.0050.0050%
B10.001.0010%

AVG(margin_%) = (50% + 10%) ÷ 2 = 30%. But the business earned 51 on 110 of sales: the true margin is 46.4%. A small order counted as much as a large one.

The fix: sum, then divide

SQL
SELECT
    SUM(margin_amount) * 100.0
      / NULLIF(SUM(sales_amount), 0) AS margin_pct
FROM fact_order_line;      -- 46.4

Store the additive numerator and denominator in the fact table. Compute the ratio at query time, at whatever level the report needs.

Non-additive measureStore instead
unit_priceline_amount and quantity (price = amount ÷ quantity)
discount %discount_amount and gross_amount

The main idea

if a number is a ratio or a distinct count, do not sum or average it. Keep its additive ingredients and recompute.

Keys · surrogate vs natural keys

Surrogate keys: why the warehouse makes its own IDs

A natural key (also called business key) is the ID that comes from the source system, such as customer_id = C-101. A surrogate key is a meaningless integer the warehouse assigns itself (1, 2, 3…). Dimensions use the surrogate key as their primary key and keep the natural key as an ordinary column.

Five problems surrogate keys solve
sourcecustomer_idname
web shop1001Maya
market stall POS1001Raj

Leafy also sells at a weekend market with its own till system. Both systems use 1001 for different people. A warehouse-generated key gives Maya and Raj different keys (1 and 7); the natural key is kept alongside with its source.

customer_keycustomer_idnamecity
1C-101MayaAustin
4C-101MayaDenver

To keep Maya’s old and new city (SCD Type 2, lesson 23) the dimension needs two rows for one customer. The natural key C-101 is no longer unique; the surrogate key is.

Source systems get migrated, IDs get reformatted (C-101 → CUST-000101), emails used as IDs change, and deleted IDs are sometimes reused. Surrogate keys insulate every fact row from these events: only the dimension’s mapping needs updating.

A sale with no known customer can point at a special row with key −1 “Unknown” (lesson 37). There is no natural key for “unknown”, but a surrogate key can represent it.

A 4-byte integer is smaller than a 36-character text ID. On a billion-row fact table, narrow integer keys save storage and make joins faster.

In practice

Generate them with an identity / sequence column, or a deterministic hash of natural key + source (common in dbt and lakehouse pipelines). Fact tables store only surrogate keys.

One exception: dates

Date dimensions commonly use a readable key such as 20260302. Dates never change meaning and never collide, so a “smart” key is safe and convenient.

The main idea

natural keys identify things in a source system; surrogate keys identify rows in the warehouse. Keep both, but join on the surrogate.

Slowly changing dimensions · the question behind every SCD type

Descriptions change. What should history show?

On 5 March 2026 Maya moved from Austin to Denver. Her first order (SO-5001, 64.00) was placed while she lived in Austin; her third (SO-5003, 18.00) after she moved. The source system simply overwrote her city — it only knows where she lives now.

The business question

“What were our sales by customer city in March?”

Two different answers are both reasonable, depending on what the business means:

Two possible answers

MeaningAustinDenver
Where the customer lived at the time of each sale (“as was”)6443
Where the customer lives today (“as is”)0107

Dimension attributes that change occasionally — a customer’s city, a product’s category, an employee’s department — are called slowly changing dimensions (SCDs). The SCD “type” is the rule you choose for how the warehouse reacts to a change. It is decided per attribute, not per table.

The main idea

an SCD type is an answer to “when this description changes, should old facts keep the old description or show the new one?”

How it works

Simple and cheap: one row per customer, nothing else changes. Fact rows keep pointing at key 1.

Consequence

History is restated. Last month’s “Austin sales” report would now show 0 for Maya, and a report run before the change can no longer be reproduced.

Use it when

The old value was a mistake (a typo in a name), or nobody needs the old value for analysis (an email address, a phone number). Many attributes in real dimensions are Type 1.

The main idea

Type 1 = overwrite. Simple, but it rewrites the past. Use it for corrections and for attributes whose history does not matter.

Slowly changing dimensions · Type 2 · the two steps, and the fact lookup

How a Type 2 load works

Step 1 and 2: maintain the dimension

SQL
-- 1. Expire current rows whose tracked attributes changed
UPDATE dim_customer AS d
SET    valid_to   = CURRENT_DATE - 1,
       is_current = FALSE
FROM   stg_customer AS s
WHERE  d.customer_id = s.customer_id
  AND  d.is_current
  AND  d.city IS DISTINCT FROM s.city;     -- tracked attribute(s)

-- 2. Insert a new current row for anyone without one
--    (brand-new customers, and the ones just expired)
INSERT INTO dim_customer
       (customer_id, name, city, valid_from, valid_to, is_current)
SELECT s.customer_id, s.name, s.city,
       CURRENT_DATE, DATE '9999-12-31', TRUE
FROM   stg_customer AS s
LEFT JOIN dim_customer AS d
       ON d.customer_id = s.customer_id AND d.is_current
WHERE  d.customer_id IS NULL;

customer_key is an identity column, so each insert gets a new key automatically. Many tools package this logic: MERGE statements, dbt snapshots, or Delta / Databricks change-data-feed patterns.

Step 3: load facts with an “as of” lookup

SQL
INSERT INTO fact_order_line (date_key, customer_key, …)
SELECT d.date_key,
       COALESCE(c.customer_key, -1),   -- unknown member
       …
FROM stg_order_lines AS s
JOIN dim_date AS d ON d.full_date = s.order_date
LEFT JOIN dim_customer AS c
       ON c.customer_id = s.customer_id
      AND s.order_date BETWEEN c.valid_from
                           AND c.valid_to;

Match on the natural key and the date range, so each sale picks up the version that was valid on its order date. SO-5001 (2 March) finds key 1; SO-5003 (9 March) finds key 4.

This uses inclusive dates: the old row ends the day before the new one starts. If you track changes with timestamps, use a half-open range (valid_from <= t AND t < valid_to) instead.

The main idea

Type 2 loading is: detect change → expire old row → insert new row → look up facts by natural key and date. Load dimensions before facts.

Three common questions

                         AND cur.is_current

Dimension techniques · dim_date · the most-used dimension in any warehouse

Date dimensions: why not just use a date column?

Databases already have a DATE type and functions such as EXTRACT(MONTH …). So why does almost every dimensional model have a dim_date table with one row per calendar day? Because the business calendar contains far more than the computer calendar.

01Things SQL cannot calculate

Fiscal periods (Leafy’s year starts 1 July), holidays and observed holidays, retail 4-4-5 calendars, promotional seasons (“Spring Sale week”), company shutdown days. These are business decisions, stored as data.

02One definition for everyone

Without a date table, each analyst writes their own “week”, “quarter” or “weekend” logic — and they will not agree. A shared dim_date gives every report the same calendar.

03Easier and faster to use

Report builders filter on readable columns (“March”, “Weekend”, “FY26 Q3”) instead of writing date functions. BI tools need it too: Power BI’s time intelligence expects a marked date table.

It also fills gaps

Transaction facts are sparse: Leafy had no orders 3–8 March. Starting a query from dim_date and LEFT JOINing the facts shows those days as zero instead of hiding them.

Separate time of day

If you need hours or minutes, use a separate dim_time_of_day (1,440 rows for minutes) rather than one row per timestamp. Combining them would create 525,600 rows per year for no benefit.

The main idea

dim_date turns the company calendar into data: one row per day, dozens of ready-made labels, shared by every fact table.

Dimension techniques · an identifier with no table of its own

Degenerate dimensions

Look again at the order number SO-5001. Its date went to dim_date, its customer to dim_customer, its products to dim_product. What is left about “the order” itself? Just the number. A dim_order table would contain only that one column — and have as many rows as the fact table.

So the order number is stored directly in the fact table, as a dimension with no dimension table. Kimball calls this a degenerate dimension (DD).

What it is useful for

SQL
-- Orders and average order value
SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT order_number)            AS orders,          -- 3
       SUM(line_amount)
         / COUNT(DISTINCT order_number)        AS avg_order_value  -- 35.67
FROM fact_order_line;

-- Group lines back into baskets: which products are bought together?
SELECT order_number, STRING_AGG(p.product_name, ' + ') AS basket
FROM fact_order_line AS f
JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
GROUP BY order_number;      -- SO-5001: Monstera + Ceramic Pot

Recognise one

  • A transaction or document identifier: order number, invoice number, receipt number, ticket ID, booking ID, payment ID.
  • Has no descriptive attributes left after the other dimensions are modelled.
  • Lives in the fact table, typically as text or a large integer. It is not a foreign key — there is nothing to join to.

Also valuable for tracing: support staff can look up an order in the source system using the number they see in a report.

If the order header does have descriptive attributes (channel, payment method, gift flag), those go into other dimensions — often a junk dimension (next lesson).

The main idea

a degenerate dimension is a transaction ID kept on the fact row. Use it to count transactions, group lines into baskets, and trace back to the source.

Drill across: combine facts through conformed dimensions

SQL
WITH sales AS (
  SELECT p.category, SUM(f.line_amount) AS sales
  FROM fact_order_line AS f
  JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
  GROUP BY p.category
), returns AS (
  SELECT p.category, SUM(r.return_amount) AS returns
  FROM fact_returns AS r
  JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = r.product_key
  GROUP BY p.category
)
SELECT COALESCE(s.category, r.category) AS category,
       s.sales, COALESCE(r.returns, 0) AS returns,
       ROUND(100.0 * COALESCE(r.returns, 0) / NULLIF(s.sales, 0), 1) AS return_rate_pct
FROM sales AS s
FULL OUTER JOIN returns AS r ON r.category = s.category;

Why it matters

Aggregate each fact separately to the same conformed attribute, then join the small results. If one Ceramic Pot (12.00) is returned: Accessories return rate = 12 ÷ 42 = 28.6%, Plants = 0%.

Never join two fact tables row to row: one order line matched to several return rows multiplies amounts (fan-out).

Typical conformed dimensions: dim_date, dim_customer, dim_product, dim_store, dim_employee. A shrunken version (e.g., dim_month, or category-only) conforms too, as long as its values match.

The main idea

conformed dimensions are what make separate fact tables comparable. They are the glue of an enterprise warehouse.

Three tests worth automating

SQL
-- 1. Grain: no duplicate rows for the declared grain (expect 0 rows)
SELECT order_number, line_number, COUNT(*) AS copies
FROM fact_order_line
GROUP BY order_number, line_number
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;

-- 2. No NULL foreign keys (expect 0)
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM fact_order_line
WHERE customer_key IS NULL OR product_key IS NULL OR date_key IS NULL;

-- 3. Reconcile with the source (expect equal totals)
SELECT (SELECT SUM(line_amount) FROM fact_order_line)  AS warehouse_total,
       (SELECT SUM(qty * price)  FROM stg_order_items) AS source_total;

Why dimensions go first

A fact row needs the dimension’s surrogate key at load time. If the dimension has not been refreshed, new customers and new versions are missing and facts would fall back to Unknown.

Tools such as dbt express this order automatically through dependencies, and provide built-in tests for uniqueness, not-null and relationships.

The main idea

staging → dimensions → facts → snapshots → tests. The grain-uniqueness test is the single most valuable check on any fact table.

Try it · decide, then reveal

Quick checks before the project

A table has one row per customer per month with the column loyalty_points_balance. What kind of fact table is it, and how should balances be totalled for a year?

Periodic snapshot. The balance is semi-additive: sum across customers for one month, but for a year take the December (closing) value or the average — never the sum of 12 months.

A support ticket moves from opened → assigned → resolved → closed, and managers want time spent in each step. Which fact table type?

Accumulating snapshot: one row per ticket, a role-playing date key per milestone, lag measures between them, updated as the ticket progresses.

A product’s category was wrong for a month because of a data-entry typo. Type 1 or Type 2?

Type 1. It is a correction; history should show the right category everywhere. Type 2 would preserve a mistake.

Every sale has an invoice number and nothing else about the invoice is descriptive. Where does invoice_number go?

On the fact table as a degenerate dimension.

A hospital visit can have several diagnoses. Revenue by diagnosis adds up to more than total revenue. Is something broken?

Not necessarily: this is a bridge table (many-to-many) and overlapping totals are expected. Either label it “revenue from visits with this diagnosis” and do not total it, or use a weighting factor so allocated revenue reconciles.

Which students were enrolled in a course but never attended a lecture? What two fact tables do you need?

A coverage factless fact (enrolment: student × course × term) and an event factless fact (attendance). Enrolled minus attended = the answer.

The main idea

if you can answer all six with a reason, you are ready to model a business from scratch.

Project: hints if you get stuck

Hint 1 · Which business processes are there?

Think in verbs: members book classes; FitHub schedules classes; people pay; members hold a plan each month; trials convert (or not). Each verb is a candidate fact table.

Hint 2 · Is a scheduled class a dimension or a fact?

Both views are defensible. A class session has descriptive attributes (studio, type, start time) — those become dimensions on the booking fact, with class_id as a degenerate dimension. The schedule itself is a coverage-style factless fact (one row per scheduled class, optionally with capacity), which is exactly what you need for “classes with no bookings”.

Hint 3 · How do I rebuild member history?

Each member starts with a TRIAL version from trial_start until the day before converted_on. Then a paid version begins. For members in member_changes.csv, the first paid version uses the old values and ends the day before changed_on; a new version with the new values starts on changed_on. Everyone’s last version is current, valid to 9999-12-31.

Hint 4 · Where do channel, status, waitlisted and guest pass go?

They are low-cardinality booking flags: a junk dimension (dim_booking_profile). Also add 0/1 measures such as attended_count and no_show_count on the fact so rates are easy sums.

Hint 5 · Which dates does a booking have?

Booked date, class date, and cancellation date — three roles of one dim_date. Bookings that were never cancelled use the −2 “Not applicable” date row, not NULL.

Hint 6 · How do I share credit for C901?

Create instructor groups: each class points at a group, and bridge_instructor_group lists the instructors in it with weights summing to 1 (0.5 and 0.5 for Dev and Kim).

Hint 7 · What member does the walk-in payment get?

P703 has no member by design, so it is not “unknown” — use the −2 Not applicable member (label it “Walk-in / non-member”). Reserve −1 Unknown for member IDs that are missing by mistake or do not match.

The main idea

every hint maps to a lesson: processes (16), factless (31), Type 2 (23–24), junk (36), role-playing (34), bridge (38), unknown members (37).

Recap · the ideas that carry everything else

What to remember when you close this course

  1. Measurements in the centre, descriptions around them. Facts measure events; dimensions give context; a star keeps every dimension one join away.
  2. Grain first, always. Say what one row is before choosing columns. Prefer atomic grain; never mix grains.
  3. Know how each number adds up. Additive, semi-additive (not over time), non-additive (store the parts).
  4. The warehouse owns its keys. Surrogate keys make history, multiple sources, and unknown members possible.
  5. Decide how history behaves. Type 1 overwrites; Type 2 adds a version so old facts keep old descriptions.
  6. Match the fact type to the rhythm. Events → transaction; levels per period → periodic snapshot; pipelines → accumulating snapshot; no numbers → factless.
  7. Use the toolbox. Date dimension, role-playing, degenerate, junk, unknown members, bridges, and conformed dimensions each solve one recurring problem.
The main idea

when in doubt, return to the questions the business asks: the measures are the numbers, the dimensions are the “by” words, and the grain is what one answer row is built from.
