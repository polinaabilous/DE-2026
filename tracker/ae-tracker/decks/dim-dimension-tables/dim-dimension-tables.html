Why this matters · the need behind the topic

Why this matters on the job

Dimensions are what users filter and group by, so their mistakes show up everywhere: last year’s sales move to a customer’s new city, a NULL key drops rows from totals, a customer in two segments doubles revenue. Surrogate keys, SCDs and the special dimension techniques prevent these problems, and interviewers ask about them in almost every modeling round.

Where you will use it

  • Customer, product and employee dimensions with history (dbt snapshots, MERGE loads).
  • Shared dim_date and conformed dimensions used by every mart.
  • BI semantic models with role-playing dates and many-to-many relationships.

What goes wrong without it

  • History overwritten: past reports change when a description changes.
  • NULL foreign keys silently drop facts from inner-joined reports.
  • Many-to-many relationships double count without a bridge.

Who expects you to know it

  • Data and analytics engineers who load and maintain dimensions.
  • BI developers wiring relationships in semantic models.
  • Interviewers: “SCD Type 1 vs 2?”, “why surrogate keys?”, “order date, ship date, delivery date?”.
On the job

Marketing asks for “revenue by customer city, as it was when they bought”. You keep city as a Type 2 attribute in dim_customer, the fact load looks up the customer version valid on the order date, and the report needs no special logic.

The main idea

well-designed dimensions keep context correct over time and make every fact easy to slice.

02

Keys · surrogate vs natural keys

Surrogate keys: why the warehouse makes its own IDs

A natural key (also called business key) is the ID that comes from the source system, such as customer_id = C-101. A surrogate key is a meaningless integer the warehouse assigns itself (1, 2, 3…). Dimensions use the surrogate key as their primary key and keep the natural key as an ordinary column.

Five problems surrogate keys solve
sourcecustomer_idname
web shop1001Maya
market stall POS1001Raj

Leafy also sells at a weekend market with its own till system. Both systems use 1001 for different people. A warehouse-generated key gives Maya and Raj different keys (1 and 7); the natural key is kept alongside with its source.

customer_keycustomer_idnamecity
1C-101MayaAustin
4C-101MayaDenver

To keep Maya’s old and new city (SCD Type 2, lesson 6) the dimension needs two rows for one customer. The natural key C-101 is no longer unique; the surrogate key is.

Source systems get migrated, IDs get reformatted (C-101 → CUST-000101), emails used as IDs change, and deleted IDs are sometimes reused. Surrogate keys insulate every fact row from these events: only the dimension’s mapping needs updating.

A sale with no known customer can point at a special row with key −1 “Unknown” (lesson 15). There is no natural key for “unknown”, but a surrogate key can represent it.

A 4-byte integer is smaller than a 36-character text ID. On a billion-row fact table, narrow integer keys save storage and make joins faster.

In practice

Generate them with an identity / sequence column, or a deterministic hash of natural key + source (common in dbt and lakehouse pipelines). Fact tables store only surrogate keys.

One exception: dates

Date dimensions commonly use a readable key such as 20260302. Dates never change meaning and never collide, so a “smart” key is safe and convenient.

On the job

Surrogate keys are generated in the dimension load (identity columns, sequences, or a hash of the natural key and version in dbt). They make Type 2 history and “Unknown” rows possible.

The main idea

natural keys identify things in a source system; surrogate keys identify rows in the warehouse. Keep both, but join on the surrogate.

03

Slowly changing dimensions · the question behind every SCD type

Descriptions change. What should history show?

On 5 March 2026 Maya moved from Austin to Denver. Her first order (SO-5001, 64.00) was placed while she lived in Austin; her third (SO-5003, 18.00) after she moved. The source system simply overwrote her city — it only knows where she lives now.

The business question

“What were our sales by customer city in March?”

Two different answers are both reasonable, depending on what the business means:

Two possible answers

MeaningAustinDenver
Where the customer lived at the time of each sale (“as was”)6443
Where the customer lives today (“as is”)0107

Dimension attributes that change occasionally — a customer’s city, a product’s category, an employee’s department — are called slowly changing dimensions (SCDs). The SCD “type” is the rule you choose for how the warehouse reacts to a change. It is decided per attribute, not per table.

On the job

Ask the business per attribute: “if a customer moves, should last year’s sales move with them?”. The answer decides Type 1 or Type 2 and belongs in the model’s documentation.

The main idea

an SCD type is an answer to “when this description changes, should old facts keep the old description or show the new one?”

04

Slowly changing dimensions · Type 1 · keep only the current value

SCD Type 1: overwrite

Maya’s dimension row
customer_keycustomer_idnamecity
1C-101MayaAustin
customer_keycustomer_idnamecity
1C-101MayaDenver

Same row, same key, city updated in place. All of Maya’s sales — past and future — now show Denver.

How it works

UPDATE dim_customer SET city = 'Denver' WHERE customer_id = 'C-101';

Simple and cheap: one row per customer, nothing else changes. Fact rows keep pointing at key 1.

Consequence

History is restated. Last month’s “Austin sales” report would now show 0 for Maya, and a report run before the change can no longer be reproduced.

Use it when

The old value was a mistake (a typo in a name), or nobody needs the old value for analysis (an email address, a phone number). Many attributes in real dimensions are Type 1.

The main idea

Type 1 = overwrite. Simple, but it rewrites the past. Use it for corrections and for attributes whose history does not matter.

Slowly changing dimensions · Type 2 · preserve full history

SCD Type 2: add a new row

Instead of overwriting, close Maya’s old row and add a new row with a new surrogate key. Each row is a version of the customer, valid for a date range.

SO-500103-021 (Maya, Austin)64.00

Sales by city now gives Austin 64, Denver 43 — the “as was” answer — with an ordinary join on customer_key.

The housekeeping columns

  • valid_from / valid_to — the date range this version was true. The open-ended current row uses a far-future date such as 9999-12-31 rather than NULL, so range checks stay simple.
  • is_current — a flag for quickly finding the latest version.
  • The natural key customer_id repeats across versions — this is exactly why dimensions need surrogate keys (lesson 3).
On the job

Type 2 dimensions are how warehouses keep “as was” history for customers, products and org structures. In dbt, snapshots generate exactly these valid_from / valid_to rows.

The main idea

Type 2 = new row per change, new surrogate key, effective dates. Old facts keep the old description automatically. It is the most important SCD type to understand.

06

Slowly changing dimensions · Type 2 · the two steps, and the fact lookup

How a Type 2 load works

Step 1 and 2: maintain the dimension

SQL
-- 1. Expire current rows whose tracked attributes changed
UPDATE dim_customer AS d
SET    valid_to   = CURRENT_DATE - 1,
       is_current = FALSE
FROM   stg_customer AS s
WHERE  d.customer_id = s.customer_id
  AND  d.is_current
  AND  d.city IS DISTINCT FROM s.city;     -- tracked attribute(s)

-- 2. Insert a new current row for anyone without one
--    (brand-new customers, and the ones just expired)
INSERT INTO dim_customer
       (customer_id, name, city, valid_from, valid_to, is_current)
SELECT s.customer_id, s.name, s.city,
       CURRENT_DATE, DATE '9999-12-31', TRUE
FROM   stg_customer AS s
LEFT JOIN dim_customer AS d
       ON d.customer_id = s.customer_id AND d.is_current
WHERE  d.customer_id IS NULL;

customer_key is an identity column, so each insert gets a new key automatically. Many tools package this logic: MERGE statements, dbt snapshots, or Delta / Databricks change-data-feed patterns.

Step 3: load facts with an “as of” lookup

SQL
INSERT INTO fact_order_line (date_key, customer_key, …)
SELECT d.date_key,
       COALESCE(c.customer_key, -1),   -- unknown member
       …
FROM stg_order_lines AS s
JOIN dim_date AS d ON d.full_date = s.order_date
LEFT JOIN dim_customer AS c
       ON c.customer_id = s.customer_id
      AND s.order_date BETWEEN c.valid_from
                           AND c.valid_to;

Match on the natural key and the date range, so each sale picks up the version that was valid on its order date. SO-5001 (2 March) finds key 1; SO-5003 (9 March) finds key 4.

This uses inclusive dates: the old row ends the day before the new one starts. If you track changes with timestamps, use a half-open range (valid_from <= t AND t < valid_to) instead.

On the job

A Type 2 load is usually a MERGE or a dbt snapshot that closes the current row and inserts a new version. Loading the fact must then look up the version that was valid on the event date.

The main idea

Type 2 loading is: detect change → expire old row → insert new row → look up facts by natural key and date. Load dimensions before facts.

07

Slowly changing dimensions · counting, “as was”, and “as is”

Querying a Type 2 dimension correctly

Three common questions
SQL
SELECT COUNT(*)                    AS rows_in_dim,      -- 4  ✗ counts versions
       COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS customers,        -- 3  ✓
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE is_current) AS current_rows  -- 3  ✓
FROM dim_customer;

A Type 2 dimension has one row per version, not per customer. Count distinct natural keys, or filter to current rows.

SQL
SELECT c.city, SUM(f.line_amount) AS revenue
FROM fact_order_line AS f
JOIN dim_customer AS c ON c.customer_key = f.customer_key
GROUP BY c.city;          -- Austin 64, Denver 43

The normal join gives history as it was at the time of each sale. This is the default behaviour of a Type 2 model.

SQL
SELECT cur.city, SUM(f.line_amount) AS revenue
FROM fact_order_line AS f
JOIN dim_customer AS hist ON hist.customer_key = f.customer_key
JOIN dim_customer AS cur  ON cur.customer_id   = hist.customer_id
                         AND cur.is_current
GROUP BY cur.city;        -- Denver 107

Hop from the historical version to the current version through the natural (or a durable) key. Some teams add “current_city” columns to every version (a Type 6 hybrid, next lesson) to make this a single join.

Point-in-time questions

“How many customers lived in Austin on 1 March?” → WHERE DATE '2026-03-01' BETWEEN valid_from AND valid_to AND city = 'Austin'.

Interview phrasing

Be ready to explain “as was” vs “as is” reporting, and why COUNT(*) on a Type 2 dimension over-counts.

On the job

The most common Type 2 query bug is counting rows instead of distinct natural keys, so one moved customer counts twice. Reviewers check for this.

The main idea

Type 2 makes “as was” the default and “as is” possible. Always count entities by natural key, not by dimension rows.

Slowly changing dimensions · 9A · worked example

Type 3: keep two views of the same sales

Leafy redraws its sales regions. Maya’s assignment changes from South to Central; Leo stays in West. Management wants to compare the same orders under both maps. Type 3 keeps an alternate assignment beside the main value, so a report can choose its lens.

Before and after the reorganisation

Statecustomer_keycustomer_idsales_regionprevious_sales_region
Before1C-101SouthNULL
After1C-101CentralSouth
After2C-102WestNULL

The key stays 1. Both Maya orders still reference that row: 64 + 18 = 82. Grouping by sales_region puts all 82 in Central. Grouping by COALESCE(previous_sales_region, sales_region) puts all 82 in South. Leo’s 25 stays in West.

The important distinction

The old-map query also puts Maya’s newer order in South. This is an alternate classification of the same facts, rather than a reconstruction of the region at each order date.

Use it for a small, agreed set of comparisons: the old organisation versus the new one, or an original category versus today’s category. If every historical assignment and its dates must remain queryable, choose a versioned design such as Type 2.

The main idea

Type 3 preserves a limited set of alternative descriptions. Choose the column that matches the report’s business question.

Slowly changing dimensions · 9B · worked example

Type 3: update safely and name the comparison

This rolling-previous implementation moves the old assignment into previous_sales_region only when the incoming assignment differs. The guard matters: replaying the same Central update should leave South in the previous column. The examples use DuckDB, a local analytical SQL database; the downloadable lab creates its tables in memory.

SQL · DuckDB
UPDATE dim_customer3
SET previous_sales_region = sales_region,
    sales_region = 'Central'
WHERE customer_id = 'C-101'
  AND sales_region IS DISTINCT FROM 'Central';
Compare the same 107 of sales under both maps
SQL · DuckDB
SELECT 'new_map' AS perspective,
       d.sales_region AS region, SUM(f.amount) AS sales
FROM fact_order3 AS f
JOIN dim_customer3 AS d USING (customer_key)
GROUP BY d.sales_region
UNION ALL
SELECT 'old_map',
       COALESCE(d.previous_sales_region, d.sales_region),
       SUM(f.amount)
FROM fact_order3 AS f
JOIN dim_customer3 AS d USING (customer_key)
GROUP BY COALESCE(d.previous_sales_region, d.sales_region)
ORDER BY perspective, region;

Expected result

perspectiveregionsales
new_mapCentral82.00
new_mapWest25.00
old_mapSouth82.00
old_mapWest25.00

A later move to East would leave Central as previous and erase South from this two-slot representation. If the requirement is specifically “before the 2026 reorganisation,” preserve a named baseline such as sales_region_before_2026_reorg once. Agree whether a column means original, previous, or a particular approved map.

In this fixture, the original assignments are known and an empty previous slot means unchanged. If an old assignment can itself be unknown, distinguish that state explicitly; blindly falling back to the current region would misrepresent the old map.

Load assumptions

The example applies one authoritative assignment per customer. Deduplicate incoming changes and define effective order before rotating values. Replaying an older source snapshot must not be treated as a new business change.

Try it: a replay, then a second change

Run the Central update twice. Maya must still be Central / South. Change the incoming value to East and run it: expect East / Central. Explain why neither version of the report can now reconstruct all historical assignments.

The main idea

A previous-value slot is limited history. A replay guard protects it, and precise column names protect its meaning.

Type 4: separate a reusable customer profile

Suppose Leafy’s large customer dimension also carries loyalty tier, age band and income band. Creating a full customer version whenever this profile changes can duplicate many stable attributes. 9C

Slowly changing dimensions · 9D · worked example

Type 4: capture the profile at the event time

  1. Prepare the event. Resolve the customer and the profile that applied when the order occurred. A historical backfill needs historical profile evidence.
  2. Find or add the combination. Look up the complete tuple. Insert an observed combination only when absent; enforce uniqueness.
  3. Write the fact. Store both keys. Validate unmatched lookups and reconcile row counts before publishing.
SQL · DuckDB
SELECT p.loyalty_tier, SUM(f.amount) AS sales
FROM fact_order4 AS f
JOIN dim_customer4 AS c USING (customer_key)
JOIN dim_profile4 AS p USING (profile_key)
GROUP BY p.loyalty_tier
ORDER BY p.loyalty_tier;

Expected sales by the profile on each order

loyalty_tiersales
Bronze89.00
Silver18.00
See the complete profile lookup and fact load

The lab provides three unique staged orders, non-null profile bands and a sequence that generates surrogate keys. It validates customer lookups before these statements. Run both inside one transaction.

SQL · DuckDB
INSERT INTO dim_profile4 (loyalty_tier, age_band, income_band)
SELECT DISTINCT s.loyalty_tier, s.age_band, s.income_band
FROM stg_order4 AS s
WHERE NOT EXISTS (
    SELECT 1 FROM dim_profile4 AS p
    WHERE p.loyalty_tier = s.loyalty_tier
      AND p.age_band = s.age_band
      AND p.income_band = s.income_band
)
ORDER BY 1, 2, 3;
SQL · DuckDB
INSERT INTO fact_order4
SELECT s.order_number, s.order_date,
       c.customer_key, p.profile_key, s.amount
FROM stg_order4 AS s
JOIN dim_customer4 AS c USING (customer_id)
JOIN dim_profile4 AS p
  ON p.loyalty_tier = s.loyalty_tier
 AND p.age_band = s.age_band
 AND p.income_band = s.income_band
WHERE NOT EXISTS (
    SELECT 1 FROM fact_order4 AS f
    WHERE f.order_number = s.order_number
);

The replay guard assumes an identical replay. If the same order ID arrives with different values, validate and apply an explicit correction policy rather than silently ignoring it.

Choose bounded bands deliberately. Three tiers × eight age bands × six income bands permits 144 combinations in this toy design; exact balances or timestamps could make combinations nearly unique per customer. Store observed tuples rather than eagerly generating every possible tuple.

What facts cannot tell you

If a profile changes between purchases, an order fact alone does not record that change. A dated customer–profile assignment history, such as a factless fact table, is needed for questions about every interval between events.

Try it: a shared profile and a backfill

Give Ana a new order with the same Silver profile: reuse key 11. Then backfill a Maya order from before the tier change: its profile must be 10, even if her current tier is Silver. Explain how you would resolve it if the source supplies only today’s profile.

The main idea

A mini-dimension stays useful when combinations are reusable and facts carry the correct event-time key.

9E

Slowly changing dimensions · 9F · worked example

Type 6: update every version, then prove both views

For an ordered, effective-dated change, detect the real difference before modifying rows. Close the old version, insert the new one, and overwrite current_city for every version of the same customer. A transaction publishes those steps together.

  1. Keep the historical column. Expired rows retain their city and surrogate key.
  2. Propagate the current column. Every C-101 row receives Denver, including key 1.
  3. Keep fact keys stable. Existing facts retain their historical keys; new or late facts use an event-date lookup.
The transactional update used in the lab

DuckDB SQL. The fixture provides a surrogate-key sequence and one deduplicated change per customer, with an effective date strictly after the existing version’s start. Detecting only changed values makes an identical replay a no-op. For multiple changes within a day, use timestamp intervals and a consistent half-open boundary rule.

SQL · DuckDB
BEGIN TRANSACTION;
-- Freeze the actual changes before expiring any rows.
CREATE OR REPLACE TEMP TABLE changed6 AS
SELECT s.*
FROM stg_city6 AS s
JOIN dim_customer6 AS d USING (customer_id)
WHERE d.is_current AND d.city IS DISTINCT FROM s.city;

UPDATE dim_customer6 AS d
SET valid_to = s.effective_date - 1, is_current = FALSE
FROM changed6 AS s
WHERE d.customer_id = s.customer_id AND d.is_current;

INSERT INTO dim_customer6
    (customer_id, name, city, current_city,
     valid_from, valid_to, is_current)
SELECT customer_id, name, city, city,
       effective_date, DATE '9999-12-31', TRUE
FROM changed6;

-- Deliberately update EVERY version of the customer.
UPDATE dim_customer6 AS d
SET current_city = s.city
FROM changed6 AS s
WHERE d.customer_id = s.customer_id;
COMMIT;
Two easy mistakes

Updating current_city only WHERE is_current leaves old facts grouped under stale “current” values. Conversely, filtering WHERE is_current after joining facts by their historical key drops Maya’s old 64 of sales: the remaining total is only 43.

Use a durable identity to group all versions. In this example customer_id is assumed stable; if a source can reassign or replace it, resolve a warehouse-managed durable identity first. Test one current row, no overlapping intervals, and a single current-city value across each customer’s versions.

The trade-off is deliberate: the historical city remains reproducible, while yesterday’s “as is” report may change after another move. Many versions also mean more rows to update. Type 2 plus a separate current-row join can be simpler when the combined view is rarely needed.

Try it: replay, failure and a third city

Replay the Denver change: no extra version should appear. Roll back a deliberately interrupted update: readers should retain the previous state. Then apply a later Seattle move: historical cities must remain Austin / Denver / Seattle, while every current_city becomes Seattle. Retroactive corrections need interval repair; do not run them blindly through this forward-change example.

The main idea

Type 6 is useful when both perspectives are common enough to justify maintaining a current attribute on all historical rows.

Dimension techniques · dim_date · the most-used dimension in any warehouse

Date dimensions: why not just use a date column?

Databases already have a DATE type and functions such as EXTRACT(MONTH …). So why does almost every dimensional model have a dim_date table with one row per calendar day? Because the business calendar contains far more than the computer calendar.

01Things SQL cannot calculate

Fiscal periods (Leafy’s year starts 1 July), holidays and observed holidays, retail 4-4-5 calendars, promotional seasons (“Spring Sale week”), company shutdown days. These are business decisions, stored as data.

02One definition for everyone

Without a date table, each analyst writes their own “week”, “quarter” or “weekend” logic — and they will not agree. A shared dim_date gives every report the same calendar.

03Easier and faster to use

Report builders filter on readable columns (“March”, “Weekend”, “FY26 Q3”) instead of writing date functions. BI tools need it too: Power BI’s time intelligence expects a marked date table.

It also fills gaps

Transaction facts are sparse: Leafy had no orders 3–8 March. Starting a query from dim_date and LEFT JOINing the facts shows those days as zero instead of hiding them.

Separate time of day

If you need hours or minutes, use a separate dim_time_of_day (1,440 rows for minutes) rather than one row per timestamp. Combining them would create 525,600 rows per year for no benefit.

On the job

Almost every warehouse has a shared dim_date generated once for many years. Fiscal calendars and holidays live there, not in each report.

The main idea

dim_date turns the company calendar into data: one row per day, dozens of ready-made labels, shared by every fact table.

Generate it once, for many years

SQL
SELECT
  CAST(TO_CHAR(d, 'YYYYMMDD') AS INT)          AS date_key,
  d::date                                      AS full_date,
  TO_CHAR(d, 'FMDay')                          AS day_name,
  CASE WHEN EXTRACT(ISODOW FROM d) IN (6, 7)
       THEN 'Weekend' ELSE 'Weekday' END       AS weekday_flag,
  EXTRACT(WEEK FROM d)                         AS iso_week,
  TO_CHAR(d, 'FMMonth')                        AS month_name,
  'Q' || EXTRACT(QUARTER FROM d)               AS quarter,
  -- fiscal year starts 1 July: shift the date forward 6 months
  'FY' || TO_CHAR(d + INTERVAL '6 months', 'YY')
     || ' Q' || EXTRACT(QUARTER FROM d + INTERVAL '6 months')
                                               AS fiscal_period
FROM generate_series(DATE '2015-01-01', DATE '2035-12-31',
                     INTERVAL '1 day') AS g(d);

Design notes

  • Key: a readable integer such as 20260302 is standard for dates (lesson 3).
  • Holidays come from a small business-maintained list joined in after generation. Note the observed holiday on Friday 3 July.
  • Labels and numbers: keep month_name for display and month_number for sorting.
  • Special rows for unknown or not-yet-happened dates (lesson 15).
  • 20 years is only about 7,300 rows — tiny. Build it far into the future so loads never fail on a missing date.
  • PostgreSQL syntax shown; other engines use a recursive CTE or a sequence generator.
The main idea

generate dim_date once with every calendar label the business uses, including fiscal periods and holidays. It is small, static, and used everywhere.

Role-playing dimensions

Query it with one alias per role

SQL
SELECT od.month_name   AS order_month,
       dd.weekday_flag AS delivered_on,
       COUNT(*)        AS orders
FROM fact_order_fulfillment AS f
JOIN dim_date AS od ON od.date_key = f.order_date_key
JOIN dim_date AS dd ON dd.date_key = f.delivered_date_key
GROUP BY od.month_name, dd.weekday_flag;

Make roles friendly

Create a view per role with renamed columns (dim_order_date with order_month, dim_delivery_date with delivery_month), so report users never see three identical “month_name” fields. In Power BI, import one role-playing table per role, or use inactive relationships with USERELATIONSHIP.

Other examples: dim_customer as buyer and gift recipient; dim_airport as origin and destination; dim_employee as sales rep and approving manager.

On the job

Order date, ship date and delivery date on one fact are modelled as three foreign keys to one date dimension. In Power BI the same idea appears as role-playing date tables or inactive relationships.

The main idea

role-playing = the same physical dimension joined several times for different meanings. Store one table; give each role its own key, alias, or view.

Dimension techniques · an identifier with no table of its own

Degenerate dimensions

Look again at the order number SO-5001. Its date went to dim_date, its customer to dim_customer, its products to dim_product. What is left about “the order” itself? Just the number. A dim_order table would contain only that one column — and have as many rows as the fact table.

So the order number is stored directly in the fact table, as a dimension with no dimension table. Kimball calls this a degenerate dimension (DD).

What it is useful for

SQL
-- Orders and average order value
SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT order_number)            AS orders,          -- 3
       SUM(line_amount)
         / COUNT(DISTINCT order_number)        AS avg_order_value  -- 35.67
FROM fact_order_line;

-- Group lines back into baskets: which products are bought together?
SELECT order_number, STRING_AGG(p.product_name, ' + ') AS basket
FROM fact_order_line AS f
JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
GROUP BY order_number;      -- SO-5001: Monstera + Ceramic Pot

Recognise one

  • A transaction or document identifier: order number, invoice number, receipt number, ticket ID, booking ID, payment ID.
  • Has no descriptive attributes left after the other dimensions are modelled.
  • Lives in the fact table, typically as text or a large integer. It is not a foreign key — there is nothing to join to.

Also valuable for tracing: support staff can look up an order in the source system using the number they see in a report.

If the order header does have descriptive attributes (channel, payment method, gift flag), those go into other dimensions — often a junk dimension (next lesson).

The main idea

a degenerate dimension is a transaction ID kept on the fact row. Use it to count transactions, group lines into baskets, and trace back to the source.

Dimension techniques · tidy up the leftover flags

Junk dimensions

Leafy orders also carry small descriptive fields: sales channel (Web, App), payment method (Card, PayPal, Gift card), whether it is a gift, and whether a coupon was used. Each has only a few possible values. Where should they go?

Three options

Four text columns repeated on every fact row. The fact table gets wide, descriptive text mixes with measures, and labels are inconsistent (“Y”, “yes”, “1”).

dim_channel (2 rows), dim_payment_method (3), dim_gift_flag (2), dim_coupon_flag (2). Correct, but four extra keys on the fact and four extra boxes cluttering the model.

How to build and load it

Small sets: generate every combination up front (a cross join of the value lists). Larger sets: add a row the first time a new combination appears. During the fact load, look up the key by matching all the columns.

What not to junk

Only low-cardinality attributes (few distinct values) that do not belong to an existing dimension. A free-text delivery note or a customer email is not junk; it belongs elsewhere or nowhere.

On the job

Junk dimensions keep facts narrow when sources send many yes/no flags (gift wrap, express, coupon used). They are typically built as the distinct combinations seen in the data.

The main idea

a junk dimension groups miscellaneous low-cardinality flags and codes into one small table of their combinations, with one key on the fact.

Dimension techniques · unknown and not-applicable members

Never leave a foreign key NULL

A fourth order arrives from Leafy’s weekend market stall: SO-5004, 30.00, no customer recorded. It is tempting to leave customer_key NULL. Watch what happens.

Compare
SQL
SELECT c.city, SUM(f.line_amount) AS revenue
FROM fact_order_line AS f
JOIN dim_customer AS c ON c.customer_key = f.customer_key
GROUP BY c.city;
-- Austin 64, Denver 43   → total 107. The 30.00 sale vanished.

An inner join drops the row: revenue silently disappears from every report joined to customers. A LEFT JOIN keeps it, but shows a blank group — and every user must remember to use LEFT JOIN.

Late-arriving dimensions: if a sale references customer C-200 before C-200 reaches the warehouse, insert an inferred row (key, natural key, “Unknown” attributes) and update it when the real data arrives — the fact keeps its key. On the job

Facts that arrive before their dimension rows (late-arriving dimensions) are common with event streams. Pointing them at an Unknown or inferred member keeps totals complete and the load re-runnable.

The main idea

every fact foreign key should point at a real dimension row. Use labelled special members (Unknown, Not applicable, Not yet) instead of NULL.

       SUM(f.line_amount)            AS revenue_from_members,
       SUM(f.line_amount * b.weight) AS revenue_allocated

JOIN dim_customer AS c ON c.customer_key = f.customer_key

From members: “how much did customers in this segment spend?” Each row is right, but Maya’s 82 is counted in both segments, so the rows must not be added up.

Allocated: weights for each group sum to 1, so totals reconcile to 107. Use it when the business wants credit shared out.

Other bridges: patients with several diagnoses, bank accounts with several holders, products with many tags, classes taught by two instructors.

On the job

Customers in several segments, accounts with several owners, and patients with several diagnoses all need a bridge. Reports that ignore it double count; reports that use weights reconcile.

The main idea

use a bridge table when one fact row relates to many members of a dimension. Decide whether you want overlapping totals or weighted allocation, and label the result clearly.

Dimension techniques · one shared definition across fact tables

Conformed dimensions

Leafy now has several fact tables: order lines, returns, daily inventory, website sessions. If each team builds its own product table, “Accessories” in the sales report may not mean the same as “Accessories” in the inventory report. A conformed dimension is one dimension — same keys, same attribute names, same values — shared by every fact table that needs it.

Drill across: combine facts through conformed dimensions

SQL
WITH sales AS (
  SELECT p.category, SUM(f.line_amount) AS sales
  FROM fact_order_line AS f
  JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
  GROUP BY p.category
), returns AS (
  SELECT p.category, SUM(r.return_amount) AS returns
  FROM fact_returns AS r
  JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = r.product_key
  GROUP BY p.category
)
SELECT COALESCE(s.category, r.category) AS category,
       s.sales, COALESCE(r.returns, 0) AS returns,
       ROUND(100.0 * COALESCE(r.returns, 0) / NULLIF(s.sales, 0), 1) AS return_rate_pct
FROM sales AS s
FULL OUTER JOIN returns AS r ON r.category = s.category;

Why it matters

Aggregate each fact separately to the same conformed attribute, then join the small results. If one Ceramic Pot (12.00) is returned: Accessories return rate = 12 ÷ 42 = 28.6%, Plants = 0%.

Never join two fact tables row to row: one order line matched to several return rows multiplies amounts (fan-out).

Typical conformed dimensions: dim_date, dim_customer, dim_product, dim_store, dim_employee. A shrunken version (e.g., dim_month, or category-only) conforms too, as long as its values match.

On the job

Conformed dimensions are what make numbers comparable across teams: one dim_customer and one dim_date shared by every fact. Platform teams often own them centrally.

The main idea

conformed dimensions are what make separate fact tables comparable. They are the glue of an enterprise warehouse.

Recap · the ideas that carry everything else

Recap: what to remember when you close this deck

  1. The warehouse owns its keys. Surrogate keys make history, multiple sources and unknown members possible.
  2. Decide how history behaves, per attribute. Type 1 overwrites; Type 2 adds a version so old facts keep old descriptions.
  3. Load Type 2 by closing the current row and inserting a new one; load facts by looking up the version valid on the event date.
  4. Count Type 2 entities with distinct natural keys, and filter is_current only when you want today’s view.
  5. Use the toolbox: a shared date dimension, role-playing keys, degenerate IDs, junk dimensions for flags, and Unknown rows instead of NULL keys.
  6. Bridges resolve many-to-many (with weights to reconcile); conformed dimensions make facts comparable.
The main idea

good dimensions are wide, readable, keyed by the warehouse, and explicit about how history is kept.
