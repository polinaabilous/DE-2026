Why this matters · the need behind the topic

Why this matters on the job

Most wrong numbers on dashboards come from facts that are aggregated the wrong way: twelve month-end balances summed into a “yearly balance”, averages of averages, or snapshot rows treated like events. Knowing measure additivity and the three fact table types prevents these errors and is a standard interview topic.

Where you will use it

  • Designing sales, inventory, subscription and fulfilment marts.
  • Defining measures in semantic models (Power BI, dbt metrics) with the right aggregation.
  • Choosing how a pipeline loads a table: append events, write snapshots, or update milestones.

What goes wrong without it

  • Balances summed over time; ratios averaged instead of recomputed.
  • Daily snapshots missing days when nothing changed, so trends break.
  • Process durations calculated from scattered event rows with fragile self-joins.

Who expects you to know it

  • Data engineers and BI developers defining measures.
  • Data engineers loading facts on a schedule.
  • Interviewers: “give an example of each fact table type”, “why is a balance semi-additive?”.
On the job

Operations wants “average days from order to delivery, by warehouse”. You choose an accumulating snapshot: one row per order, a date key per milestone, lag measures between them, updated as each milestone arrives. The average is then one GROUP BY.

The main idea

measures must be aggregated the way their meaning allows, and the fact table type must match the rhythm of the process.

02

Measures · which numbers can be added up, and along which dimensions

Three kinds of measures

Not every number in a fact table can simply be summed. Classify each measure by whether SUM gives a meaningful answer across all, some, or none of the dimensions.

KindCan be summed across…Leafy examplesHow to aggregate
Additiveevery dimension (products, customers, dates…)quantity, line_amount, discount_amount, shipping_fee (at the right grain)SUM, freely
Semi-additivesome dimensions, but not timeunits on hand in a warehouse; a customer’s gift-card balanceSUM across products or warehouses; across time use the period-end value, or an average
Non-additiveno dimensionunit_price, discount %, margin %, conversion rate, distinct customer countsStore the additive parts; compute the ratio after summing

Why it matters

A BI tool will happily SUM any number column. If a measure is semi- or non-additive, the default total on a report is wrong unless the model or the measure definition prevents it.

Best practice

Prefer additive measures. Store numerators and denominators (margin_amount, sales_amount) rather than ratios. Document semi-additive measures and define their aggregation explicitly in the semantic layer.

On the job

Semantic models (Power BI, dbt metrics, Fabric) need to know how each measure aggregates. Marking a balance as semi-additive is how dashboards avoid summing twelve month-end balances into one meaningless number.

The main idea

additive: sum anything. Semi-additive: sum anything except time. Non-additive: never sum — rebuild from additive parts.

03

Measures · stock levels and balances

Semi-additive measures in action

Leafy records how many Monsteras are on the shelf at the end of each day in each warehouse. That number is a level (a state at a moment), not an activity (something that happened during the period).

datewarehouseunits_on_handunits_sold
03-01Austin100
03-01Denver40
03-02Austin91
03-02Denver40
03-03Austin90
03-03Denver31
Ask a question

“How many Monsteras did we have on 3 March?” Austin 9 + Denver 3 = 12. Summing a level across warehouses (or products) on the same day is correct.

“How many Monsteras did Austin have in the first three days of March?” SUM gives 10 + 9 + 9 = 28. Nonsense: there were never 28 plants. Use the last value (9, the closing stock) or the average (9.33).

units_sold is an activity, so it is fully additive: Austin sold 1 and Denver sold 1 → 2 over the three days. Snapshots often carry both kinds of measure side by side.

SQL
-- Closing stock per warehouse for March: take the last day of the period
SELECT w.warehouse_name, SUM(f.units_on_hand) AS closing_units
FROM fact_inventory_daily AS f
JOIN dim_warehouse AS w ON w.warehouse_key = f.warehouse_key
JOIN dim_date      AS d ON d.date_key      = f.date_key
WHERE d.full_date = DATE '2026-03-31'      -- one day only: no summing over time
GROUP BY w.warehouse_name;
On the job

Inventory levels, account balances, open tickets and subscriber counts are all semi-additive; finance and operations reports use the closing or average value over time.

The main idea

balances, stock levels, headcounts and queue lengths are semi-additive: sum them across things, but pick one point in time (or average) across time.

Measures · ratios, percentages, prices, and distinct counts

Non-additive measures: store the parts

The trap: averaging percentages

ordersales_amountmargin_amountmargin_%
A100.0050.0050%
B10.001.0010%

AVG(margin_%) = (50% + 10%) ÷ 2 = 30%. But the business earned 51 on 110 of sales: the true margin is 46.4%. A small order counted as much as a large one.

The fix: sum, then divide

SQL
SELECT
    SUM(margin_amount) * 100.0
      / NULLIF(SUM(sales_amount), 0) AS margin_pct
FROM fact_order_line;      -- 46.4

Store the additive numerator and denominator in the fact table. Compute the ratio at query time, at whatever level the report needs.

Non-additive measureStore instead
unit_priceline_amount and quantity (price = amount ÷ quantity)
discount %discount_amount and gross_amount
conversion ratesessions and orders
count of distinct customerskeep customer_key on the fact and COUNT(DISTINCT) at query time — daily distinct counts cannot be added into a monthly one
On the job

Conversion rates, margins and averages are stored as their parts (numerator and denominator) so any report can recompute the ratio at its own level. Averaging stored ratios is a frequent review finding.

The main idea

if a number is a ratio or a distinct count, do not sum or average it. Keep its additive ingredients and recompute.

Fact table types · a map before the details

Three types of fact table

Business processes measure things in different rhythms. Some record each event; some take a regular picture of a level; some follow one item through a series of steps. Each rhythm gets its own kind of fact table.

TransactionPeriodic snapshotAccumulating snapshot
GrainOne row per eventOne row per thing per periodOne row per process instance, for its whole life
Leafy examplefact_order_linefact_inventory_dailyfact_order_fulfillment
Rows appearWhen an event happensEvery period, even with no activityOnce, when the process starts
Rows change?Never (insert only)Never (new set each period)Yes — updated as milestones happen
DatesOne event dateOne snapshot dateSeveral milestone dates (role-playing)
Typical measuresAdditive amounts and countsSemi-additive levels + period activityDurations between steps, milestone counts
Answers“What happened?”“What was the level at the end of each period?”“How long does each step take? Where are things stuck?”

A fourth, the factless fact table (lesson 10), has no numeric measure at all. It can follow any of these rhythms.

On the job

Picking the fact table type is part of every new mart design. Orders and clicks are transactions; daily inventory and month-end balances are periodic snapshots; order fulfilment and loan applications are accumulating snapshots.

The main idea

events → transaction fact; regular measurements of a level → periodic snapshot; a pipeline with milestones → accumulating snapshot. This comparison is a very common interview question.

06

Fact table types · one row per event

Transaction fact tables

Leafy’s order lines

fact_order_linefact
  • date_keyFK
  • customer_keyFK
  • product_keyFK
  • warehouse_keyFK
  • promotion_keyFK
  • order_numberDD
  • line_numberDD
  • quantityM
  • line_amountM
  • discount_amountM

Grain: one row per product on a customer order.

Characteristics

  • Most common and most detailed. Sales, payments, clicks, log-ins, sensor readings.
  • Insert-only. An event that happened does not change. Corrections arrive as new rows (e.g., a return or a reversal).
  • Sparse. Rows exist only when something happens. Leafy had no orders from 3 to 8 March, so there are no rows for those days — to show zeros, join from dim_date.
  • Additive measures, so almost any roll-up works.

Limitation: to know a level (“how much stock on 31 March?”) you would have to replay every event since the beginning. That is what snapshots solve.

The main idea

transaction facts record what happened, one event per row, and never change. Start here for most business processes.

Fact table types · a regular picture of a level

Periodic snapshot fact tables

Leafy’s daily inventory

fact_inventory_dailyfact
  • snapshot_date_keyFK
  • product_keyFK
  • warehouse_keyFK
  • units_on_handM
  • units_receivedM
  • units_soldM
  • inventory_valueM

Grain: one row per product per warehouse per day.

dateproductwarehouseon_handsold
03-02MonsteraAustin91
03-03MonsteraAustin90
03-04MonsteraAustin90

Characteristics

  • Dense. A row exists for every product × warehouse × day, even when nothing happened (highlighted rows). “No activity” is itself information.
  • Predictable size: products × warehouses × days. 2,000 products × 2 warehouses × 365 days ≈ 1.5 million rows a year.
  • Semi-additive levels (on hand, balance) sit next to additive activity for the period (received, sold).
  • Common periods: daily inventory, monthly account balances, month-end subscription status, weekly headcount.

Built by taking the current state at the end of each period, or by aggregating transactions — a snapshot complements a transaction fact table; it does not replace it.

On the job

Daily or monthly snapshots (stock on hand, account balances, active subscriptions) are built by a scheduled job that writes one row per thing per period, even when nothing changed.

The main idea

periodic snapshots answer “what was the level at the end of each period?” cheaply. Remember: their level measures cannot be summed across time.

Fact table types · one row that follows a process to the end

Accumulating snapshot fact tables

Leafy wants to know how long fulfilment takes. An order passes through milestones: placed → shipped → delivered. An accumulating snapshot has one row per order and a date column for each milestone, filled in as the order progresses. Step through SO-5001:

Timeline for SO-5001
order_numberorder_dateshipped_datedelivered_datedays_to_shipdays_ship_to_deliveris_delivered
SO-50012026-03-02Not yetNot yetNULLNULLNo

Row inserted when the order is placed. Future milestones point at a special date row meaning “not yet happened” (the Dimension tables deck).

order_numberorder_dateshipped_datedelivered_datedays_to_shipdays_ship_to_deliveris_delivered
SO-50012026-03-022026-03-03Not yet1NULLNo

The same row is updated: shipped date filled, days_to_ship = 1.

order_numberorder_dateshipped_datedelivered_datedays_to_shipdays_ship_to_deliveris_delivered
SO-50012026-03-022026-03-032026-03-0613Yes

Updated again. The row is now complete; total order-to-door time = 4 days.

Characteristics

  • The only fact type whose rows are routinely updated.
  • Several dates, all pointing at dim_date in different roles (the Dimension tables deck).
  • Measures are usually lags (durations between milestones) and milestone counts.

Questions it answers

“Average days from order to delivery by warehouse.” “How many orders are shipped but not delivered right now?” (shipped_date known AND is_delivered = 'No'). “Which step is the bottleneck this month?”

Other examples: insurance claims, loan applications, hiring pipelines, support tickets.

On the job

Fulfilment pipelines, claims processing and hiring funnels are modelled as accumulating snapshots; the row is updated as each milestone happens, which is why these tables are often loaded with MERGE.

The main idea

accumulating snapshots model a pipeline with a clear start, defined milestones, and an end. One row per item, updated in place.

Fact table types · recording that something happened, or could have

Factless fact tables

Some events have nothing to measure except that they happened. A factless fact table has foreign keys (and maybe degenerate dimensions) but no numeric measure. You analyse it by counting rows.

01Event tracking: workshop attendance

fact_workshop_attendancefact
  • date_keyFK
  • customer_keyFK
  • workshop_keyFK
  • store_keyFK

Grain: one row per customer attending a workshop.

SQL
SELECT w.workshop_name, COUNT(*) AS attendees
FROM fact_workshop_attendance AS f
JOIN dim_workshop AS w ON w.workshop_key = f.workshop_key
GROUP BY w.workshop_name;

Leafy runs repotting workshops. Each attendance is an event with no amount; COUNT(*) is the measure.

02Coverage: what was possible

fact_promotion_coveragefact
  • date_keyFK
  • product_keyFK
  • promotion_keyFK

Grain: one row per product per day it is on promotion.

SQL
-- Promoted products that did NOT sell during the promotion
SELECT DISTINCT p.product_name
FROM fact_promotion_coverage AS c
JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = c.product_key
WHERE c.promotion_key = 12            -- Spring Sale
  AND NOT EXISTS (
        SELECT 1 FROM fact_order_line AS f
        WHERE f.product_key = c.product_key
          AND f.date_key    = c.date_key);

A sales table only records what did sell. To find what did not happen, you need a record of what could have — eligibility, schedules, assignments.

On the job

Attendance, sign-ins, product views without a price and “promotion coverage” are factless facts. They answer “what happened” and, with a coverage table, “what could have happened but did not”.

The main idea

no measure does not mean no value: factless facts record events (attendance, registrations) or coverage (eligibility, schedules) so you can count them and find what is missing.

Recap · the ideas that carry everything else

Recap: what to remember when you close this deck

  1. Know how each number adds up. Additive sums everywhere; semi-additive not over time; non-additive never: store the parts.
  2. A balance or stock level is semi-additive: sum across products or accounts, but over time take the closing value or the average.
  3. Match the fact type to the rhythm. Events → transaction; levels per period → periodic snapshot; pipelines with milestones → accumulating snapshot.
  4. An accumulating snapshot is updated as milestones happen and carries one date key per milestone.
  5. A factless fact records events or coverage with no measure; count its rows.
The main idea

every fact table has a declared grain, a rhythm, and a known way each measure aggregates.
