Why this matters · the need behind the topic

Why this matters on the job

Business users want the same answer to the same question, every time, from every report. Dimensional modeling is how data teams arrange warehouse tables so that “revenue by category by month” is one simple query and totals do not double count. It starts with two ideas: facts versus dimensions, and the grain.

Where you will use it

  • Designing Gold layers and marts: fact and dimension tables.
  • Power BI and Fabric semantic models, which are built for star schemas.
  • dbt marts (fct_ and dim_ models) reviewed by your team.

What goes wrong without it

  • Reports query the normalised app database and every analyst joins it differently.
  • Mixed grains: an order-level fee on line rows inflates totals.
  • One wide table per dashboard, each with its own definition of “customer”.

Who expects you to know it

  • Data and data engineers building warehouses and marts.
  • BI developers building semantic models.
  • Interviewers: “OLTP vs OLAP?”, “fact vs dimension?”, “what is the grain?”.
On the job

A stakeholder asks for “a dashboard of orders by product and region”. Before writing SQL you name the business process (orders), declare the grain (one row per order line), list the dimensions (date, product, customer, region) and the facts (quantity, amount). Everything after that is mechanical.

The main idea

dimensional modeling turns raw app tables into trusted, consistent, fast reporting, and it starts with a clear grain.

Start here · a 60-second refresher

Before we start: five words you already need

Dimensional modeling is built on ordinary database ideas. If these five feel familiar, you are ready. If one is new, read its line twice — it will come up constantly.

WordMeaningTiny example
Table, row, columnA table stores one kind of thing. Each row is one item; each column is one property.A products table: one row per product, with columns name and price.
Primary key (PK)A column whose value uniquely identifies each row in its table.product_id = P-1 identifies exactly one product.
Foreign key (FK)A column that stores another table’s primary key, to point at a row there.An order line stores product_id = P-1 to say which product was bought.
JOINCombine rows from two tables where the keys match.Join order lines to products to see the product name next to each sale.
GROUP BY + SUMCollapse rows into groups and add up a number per group.SUM(amount) … GROUP BY category gives revenue per category.

What this course adds

How to arrange tables so that those joins and sums answer business questions correctly — and keep answering them correctly as the business changes.

How to read the diagrams

Table boxes list their columns. Small tags explain each column’s role: PKFKM measure, and others introduced as we go.

The main idea

you do not need advanced SQL. You need to be comfortable reading a join and a GROUP BY; the rest is design thinking, which this course teaches.

Why dimensional modeling · two very different jobs

The problem: app databases are built for writing

Leafy’s website runs on an operational database (also called OLTP, online transaction processing). It is designed so the app can save an order quickly and never store the same fact twice. To do that, information is split into many small tables. This splitting is called normalization.

Great for the app

Changing a city name means updating one row in cities. Saving an order touches only a few rows. Nothing is duplicated, so nothing can get out of sync.

Painful for analysis

“Revenue by category and customer city” needs seven joins across eight tables. Status is a cryptic code. Each analyst may write the joins a little differently and get a different number.

On the job

When a new analytics project starts, the first thing you usually get is read access to the app database: dozens of normalised tables. Explaining why reports should not query them directly, and what you will build instead, is a conversation data engineers have with every stakeholder.

The main idea

operational databases are optimised for recording one transaction at a time. Analytics needs the opposite: reading millions of rows and slicing them by many descriptions. That is the gap dimensional modeling fills.

04

Why dimensional modeling · the core idea

Dimensional modeling in one sentence

Store each business process as a table of measurements, surrounded by tables of descriptions you can filter and group by.

01Fact tables hold measurements

Each row records a business event — a sale, a shipment, a class booking — with its numbers: quantity, amount, duration. Fact tables are long and narrow: many rows, few columns.

02Dimension tables hold descriptions

Each row describes a “who, what, where, when”: a customer, a product, a date, a warehouse. Dimension tables are short and wide: fewer rows, many descriptive columns.

03Keys connect them

Every fact row stores a key for each dimension that describes it. To answer a question you join the fact table to the dimensions you need, then group and sum.

The approach was popularised by Ralph Kimball in The Data Warehouse Toolkit (1996, third edition 2013) and is still the most common way analytics data is organised — in warehouses such as Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks and Fabric, and in BI tools such as Power BI and Tableau.

The main idea

measurements in the middle, descriptions around the edge. Every technique in this course is a refinement of that one idea.

05

Building blocks · measurements vs context

Facts vs dimensions

A fact is a measurement of an event

Something happened in the business, and we measured it. Facts are almost always numbers, and usually you want to add them up.

Leafy examples: quantity = 2, line_amount = 24.00, shipping_fee = 8.00.

A dimension gives that event its context

Dimensions answer who, what, where, when, how, and why. They are mostly text, and you use them to filter, group, and label results.

Leafy examples: the product Ceramic Pot in category Accessories, the customer Maya in Austin, the date Monday 2 March 2026.

Sort each column from Leafy’s order lines:

ColumnFact or dimension?Why
line_amountFact (measure)A number produced by the sale; summing it is meaningful.
quantityFact (measure)A count of units; summing it gives units sold.
product name, categoryDimension attributeText that describes what was sold; you group by it.
customer cityDimension attributeDescribes who / where; you filter or group by it.
order_dateDimension (date)Describes when; you group by month, weekday, quarter.
product weight in kgDimension attributeA number, but it describes the product rather than measuring the sale. Test: does it change from sale to sale?
On the job

In Fabric, Snowflake or a dbt project the same split appears as fct_ and dim_ tables. Power BI semantic models expect exactly this shape.

The main idea

ask “is this a measurement taken when the event happened, or a description of something involved in it?” Numbers that describe a thing (weight, list price) usually belong in the dimension.

06

Building blocks · a habit that makes design easy

Hear the facts and dimensions in a question

Business questions almost always follow the same shape: a number, then “by” one or more descriptions, sometimes with a filter. The number is a fact; the “by” words and filters are dimensions.

Business questionFact (the number)Dimensions (by / for / where)
Revenue by category by monthrevenue = SUM(line_amount)product → category; date → month
Units sold in Denver last weekSUM(quantity)customer → city; date → week
Average order value by marketing channelSUM(amount) ÷ COUNT(orders)order profile → channel
How many orders shipped late from each warehouse?COUNT of late orderswarehouse; date (ship vs promised)

Why this matters

When you interview stakeholders, write their questions down and underline the numbers and the “by” words. The numbers become columns in fact tables; the “by” words become dimension tables and their attributes.

A useful rule of thumb

If people say “by X” or “for each X” or “only X”, then X belongs in a dimension. If they say “how much” or “how many”, that is a fact.

On the job

In requirement meetings, write down the questions word for word and underline the measures and the “by” words. That list becomes your first draft of the fact and dimension tables.

The main idea

a dimensional model is simply the shape of the questions people ask: measures to aggregate, descriptions to slice by.

Building blocks · what goes in, what stays out

Anatomy of a fact table

Leafy’s order-line fact table

fact_order_linefact
  • date_keyFK
  • customer_keyFK
  • product_keyFK
  • warehouse_keyFK
  • order_numberDD
  • line_numberDD
  • quantityM
  • unit_priceM
  • line_amountM
  • discount_amountM

One row per order line. Four rows for our sample orders.

Three kinds of column

  • Foreign keys (FK) — one per dimension that describes the event. Together they say who, what, where, when.
  • Measures (M) — the numbers recorded by the event. These are what you SUM, COUNT, or AVG.
  • Degenerate dimensions (DD) — transaction identifiers like the order number. The Dimension tables deck explains these.

What stays out: descriptive text. The product name is not repeated on every sale row; it lives once in the product dimension and is reached through product_key.

Fact tables are the biggest tables in a warehouse — often billions of rows — so they are kept narrow: integer keys and numbers.

The main idea

a fact row = keys to its context + the numbers measured. If you find yourself adding a text description to a fact table, it probably belongs in a dimension.

Design habits

  • Surrogate key (PK) — a warehouse-made number such as 1, 2, 3. The Dimension tables deck explains why.
  • Natural key (NK) — the ID from the source system, kept so you can trace a row back.
  • Flattened hierarchies — product → subcategory → category all sit in one row, even though “Plants” repeats. Repeating short text is cheap; extra joins are not.
  • Readable labels, not codes — store “Yes / No” or “Pet safe / Not pet safe” instead of 1 / 0, and “Shipped” instead of status code 3. Report labels come straight from these columns.
  • Many attributes — dimensions often have dozens of columns. Each one is a new way to slice the facts.
The main idea

a good dimension reads like a well-labelled spreadsheet: one row per thing, every useful description as its own column, nothing hidden behind codes or extra joins.

Building blocks · fact in the centre, dimensions around it

The star schema

Put the fact table in the middle and connect each dimension to it with a single key. Drawn on a page, it looks like a star — hence the name. Compare this with the eight-table source model from lesson 4.

Leafy sales star

Every dimension joins directly to the fact table with one key. Tags: PK primary key · FK foreign key · DD degenerate dimension · NK natural key · M measure.

Each dimension is exactly one join away from the facts. Dimensions do not join to each other; every path goes through the centre.

On the job

Gold-layer marts and BI semantic models are almost always stars: one fact per business process, joined to shared dimensions by surrogate keys.

The main idea

one fact table + one join per dimension. That predictability is what makes star schemas easy to query, easy to explain, and fast.

Building blocks · star vs snowflake vs source model

Why analytics prefers a star

Compare two ways to store the product hierarchy
dim_productdimension
  • product_keyPK
  • product_name
  • subcategory
  • category

One table. “Revenue by category” is one join from the fact table. “Plants” repeats on several rows — that is fine.

dim_productdimension
  • product_keyPK
  • product_name
  • subcategory_keyFK
dim_subcategorydimension
  • subcategory_keyPK
  • subcategory
  • category_keyFK
dim_categorydimension
  • category_keyPK
  • category

Three tables. Less repetition, but “revenue by category” now needs three joins, and every user must know the chain.

Simple to understand

Business users can see the whole model on one page. Table names match how people talk: sales, products, customers, dates.

Simple to query

Every question has the same shape: join the fact to a few dimensions, filter, group, sum. Fewer joins also means fewer ways to make mistakes.

Fast

Engines and BI tools are optimised for this shape (“star join” plans, columnar compression of repeated text). Power BI’s own guidance recommends star schemas.

A snowflake schema normalizes dimensions into sub-tables. It is not wrong, and sometimes useful for very large shared hierarchies, but it gives up simplicity for storage savings that rarely matter today. A one big table (everything flattened into the fact) is easy to query, but repeats descriptions on every row and makes history and reuse hard; teams often build it from a star.

On the job

A recurring design review question is “why not one big table?” or “why not keep it normalised?”. The answers on this slide (fewer, predictable joins; consistent attributes; BI-friendly) are the ones teams use.

The main idea

prefer a star by default: flatten hierarchies into the dimension and keep every dimension one join from the facts.

Building blocks · join, filter, group, aggregate

Every star query has the same shape

Revenue by category for March 2026

SQL
SELECT
    p.category,
    SUM(f.line_amount) AS revenue,
    SUM(f.quantity)    AS units
FROM fact_order_line AS f
JOIN dim_product AS p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
JOIN dim_date    AS d ON d.date_key    = f.date_key
WHERE d.year = 2026
  AND d.month_name = 'March'
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY revenue DESC;
categoryrevenueunits
Plants65.002
Accessories42.005

Read it as a recipe

  1. FROM the fact table — start from the measurements.
  2. JOIN each dimension you need, on its key.
  3. WHERE on dimension attributes — the filters.
  4. GROUP BY dimension attributes — the “by” words.
  5. SUM / COUNT the measures.

Check it: Plants = Monstera 40 + Snake Plant 25 = 65. Accessories = Ceramic Pot 24 + Fertilizer 18 = 42. Total 107, matching lesson 3.

BI tools such as Power BI generate exactly this query when someone drags “category” and “revenue” onto a chart.

On the job

Once the model is a star, every report and ad-hoc question is the same shape of SQL. That is what makes self-service BI and semantic layers possible.

The main idea

if your model is a proper star, every business question becomes the same five-step query. That consistency is the real payoff.

Grain · what does one row represent?

Grain: the most important decision

The grain of a fact table is a precise statement of what one row represents.

Grain statementExample fact tableRows for Leafy’s sample
One row per orderfact_order3 (SO-5001, SO-5002, SO-5003)
One row per order line (product within an order)fact_order_line4
One row per product per warehouse per dayfact_inventory_dailyproducts × warehouses × days
One row per customer per dayfact_customer_daily_activitycustomers × active days
One row per website sessionfact_web_sessionone per visit

Why it comes first

The grain decides which dimensions can attach to a row and which measures make sense on it. A product can describe an order line, but not a whole order that contains three products.

How to say it well

Write it in business words, as a sentence, before choosing any columns: “One row per product on a customer order.” If two people read it and imagine different rows, it is not precise enough.

On the job

The grain statement goes into the model’s documentation (a dbt model description, for example) and is enforced with a uniqueness test on the grain columns.

The main idea

if you remember one thing from this course: before designing a fact table, finish the sentence “one row in this table represents one …”. Interviewers ask this constantly.

Grain · atomic, declared first, never mixed

Three rules for grain, and the classic mistake

01Prefer the atomic grain

Store the most detailed level the source provides (order line, not order; individual event, not daily total). It answers the widest range of questions and survives new requirements. Build summaries on top if speed is needed.

02Declare it before columns

Write the grain sentence first. Then a dimension is allowed only if it has a single value for that row; a measure only if it is true for exactly that row.

03Never mix grains

Every row in one fact table must represent the same kind of thing. Facts recorded at a different level go in a different fact table.

The mistake: order-level shipping on line rows

order_numberlineline_amountshipping_fee
SO-5001140.008.00
SO-5001224.008.00
SO-5002125.005.00
SO-5003118.000.00

The shipping fee belongs to the order, but it was copied onto each line. SUM(shipping_fee) returns 21; the true total is 13 (8 + 5 + 0).

Two correct fixes

Allocate it down to the line grain, for example by each line’s share of the order amount: Monstera gets 8 × 40/64 = 5.00, Ceramic Pot 8 × 24/64 = 3.00. Now every column is true for its row, and sums are correct.

Or keep a separate order-grain fact table (fact_order) for order-level measures such as shipping fee and order-level discounts.

Either way, document the choice so nobody adds the order-level number back onto lines later.

On the job

The classic production bug is a header-level amount (shipping fee, order discount) copied onto every line row. Totals look fine for single-line orders and are wrong for the rest, so it can go unnoticed for weeks.

The main idea

double counting almost always comes from a measure stored at the wrong grain. Ask “is this number true for exactly one row?”

Grain · how every dimensional model is designed

The four-step design process

Kimball’s four steps give you an order to make decisions in. They work for a one-table project and for an enterprise warehouse, and they are a great structure for answering “design a data model for…” interview questions.

01
Choose the business processa verb: take orders, ship parcels, book classes

An operational activity that generates events you can measure. One process → (usually) one fact table.

02
Declare the grain“one row per order line”

The precise meaning of one row. Decided before anything else; it constrains the next two steps.

03
Identify the dimensionswho, what, where, when, how, why

Descriptions that have exactly one value for a row at this grain: date, customer, product, warehouse.

04
Identify the factsthe numbers measured

Measurements true at this grain: quantity, line amount, discount. Anything at another grain goes elsewhere.

Leafy, worked through

Process: customer orders. Grain: one row per product on an order. Dimensions: order date, customer, product, warehouse, promotion. Facts: quantity, unit price, line amount, discount amount.

Start from requirements and data together

Collect the business questions (lesson 7) and look at what the source systems actually record. The best design satisfies the questions using data that really exists.

On the job

Use the four steps as the agenda for a modeling workshop: process, grain, dimensions, facts. It keeps the conversation on business questions instead of source table names.

The main idea

process → grain → dimensions → facts. Say these four steps out loud in any modeling interview and you will already sound structured.

Recap · the ideas that carry everything else

Recap: what to remember when you close this deck

  1. App databases are built for writing; analytics needs a shape built for reading and aggregating.
  2. Measurements in the centre, descriptions around them. Facts measure events; dimensions give context.
  3. A star keeps every dimension one join away from the fact, so every report query has the same simple shape.
  4. Grain first, always. Say what one row is before choosing columns. Prefer atomic grain; never mix grains.
  5. Design with four steps: business process, grain, dimensions, facts.
The main idea

when in doubt, return to the questions the business asks: the measures are the numbers, the dimensions are the “by” words, and the grain is what one answer row is built from.
